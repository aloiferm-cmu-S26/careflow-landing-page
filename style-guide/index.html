Style Guide

CareFlow helps emergency room nurses chart while they talk to patients. This guide shows how the app looks and why. Every example on this page is drawn with the real CSS shown under it, and the colors and sizes come from the same tokens the app uses.

  • Calm. Night shifts are long. Soft backgrounds, few colors, one dark sidebar.
  • Safe. Warnings use warm colors and always come with words, never color alone.
  • Nurse in control. Nothing reaches the chart until the nurse confirms it.
  • Glanceable. Status dots and counts tell the shift's state in a second.

Logo

A white medical cross on a teal tile, next to the word CareFlow. The tile is 20px with 5px corners. In a patient record the word changes to "Patient record" so the nurse always knows where they are.

CareFlow Patient record CareFlow

Keep at least 8px of space around the logo. Do not recolor the tile or stretch the cross. 


        


        
        

          
Color

          

            Spruce greens and a warm off-white keep the screen calm. Teal is the brand color. Four
            signal colors show patient status and safety. All colors are CSS custom properties in
            src/pages/app/app.css, so a screen never uses a raw hex value.

Page layout and spacing

          

            CareFlow is designed for a tablet on a wall arm or a cart: iPad landscape, 1194 × 834,
            minimum 1024px wide. Every screen uses the same frame: a 64px top bar with the patient
            header, a 196px dark workspace rail on the left, the main content, and an optional 328px
            column on the right (the Live Chart conversation).

Corners and depth

          

            Radius 8px for buttons and inputs, 10px for cards, 12 to 14px for overlays, full pill
            for chips and tags. Only floating things have a shadow: the Companion button, toasts,
            menus and dialogs.
          

          

        


        
        

          
Buttons

          

            One primary button per screen area, for the action that moves the flow forward. Labels
            are verbs that say what happens: "Confirm", "Save to chart", "Send to care team". Every
            button is at least 40px tall, a comfortable touch target with gloves.

Icons and status

            SVG. An icon always sits next to a word, except the bell and the close button, which

Menus

          

            The workspace rail is the main menu. On the dashboard it has one item. Inside a patient
            record it shows the three tools for that patient, with "My patients" as the way back.
            The bell opens a dropdown list of notifications; tapping one goes to the screen it is
            about.

Forms

          

            CareFlow has few forms because most input is spoken. The fields that exist: the
            Companion question box, the editor for a chart card, and record checkboxes when saving
            history. Every field has a label, visible or for screen readers.

Dialogs and overlays

          

            Four kinds, from strongest to lightest. A modal dialog stops everything
            for a decision that changes the chart. A side sheet (the Companion)
            sits beside the work without blocking it. A toast tells the nurse that
            something happened elsewhere. A popover explains one item and closes
            when you tap outside. All of them close with Escape.

Design patterns

          

            These are the interaction patterns the MVP uses, where each one appears, and why we
            chose it. The first one is the navigation pattern.
          

          

            

              
Navigation

              
Persistent sidebar navigation

              

                Where: the dark workspace rail on every screen. Dashboard shows "My
                patients"; a patient record adds Patient History, Medication Review and Live Chart.
              

              

                Why: nurses switch tools for the same patient many times. A rail
                that never moves keeps every tool one tap away and always shows where you are
                (highlighted item). "My patients" with a back arrow is the way out.
              

              

                Reference:
                Material Design 3, Navigation drawer
              

            

            

              
Data

              
Filter chips over a data table

              

                Where: My patients dashboard (All, Waiting, In progress, Awaiting
                review, Done).
              

              

                Why: one tap narrows the shift list to what needs attention, and
                each chip shows its count so the nurse sees the workload before tapping.
              

              

                Reference:
                Material Design 3, Chips (filter chips)
              

            

            

              
Assistance

              
Side sheet assistant

              

                Where: the Nurse Companion, opened from the floating "Ask
                Companion" button on any screen.
              

              

                Why: the nurse can ask about a drug without losing the chart behind
                it. It is non-modal, can be minimized, and every answer shows its source.
              

              

                Reference:
                Material Design 3, Side sheets
              

            

            

              
Safety

              
Review before saving (modal confirmation)

              

                Where: Patient History "Add to chart" and Live Chart "Review and
                Save".
              

              

                Why: saving changes the legal patient record. A modal dialog lists
                exactly what will be saved and lets the nurse go back. Nothing is saved by accident.
              

              

                Reference:
                Nielsen Norman Group, Modal and Nonmodal Dialogs
              

            

            

              
Feedback

              
Notification toast with badge

              

                Where: when Dr. Patel replies to a medication review: a toast under
                the bell plus a count badge on it.
              

              

                Why: the reply arrives while the nurse is busy elsewhere. The toast
                is visible but does not block, and the badge keeps it findable later.
              

              

                Reference:
                Material Design 3, Badges
                and
                Snackbar
              

            

            

              
Onboarding

              
Empty state with one clear action

              

                Where: Patient History before any records ("Ask Alex to tap their
                phone on this tablet").
              

              

                Why: an empty screen explains what will appear and gives the single
                next step, so the nurse is never stuck.
              

              

                Reference:
                Nielsen Norman Group, Designing Empty States
              

            

            

              
Input

              
Draft, confirm or edit (human in the loop)

              
Where: Live Chart cards (Symptoms, Findings, Plan).

              

                Why: CareFlow drafts the chart from speech, but speech can be
                misheard. Each card starts as a draft and the nurse confirms or edits it in place.
                The page counts "confirmed" and "draft" so nothing is missed.
              

              

                Reference:
                Material Design 3, Cards
